# Grammar and diction rules (Munindar P. Singh, verbatim where quoted)

Source: https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/mpsingh/local/advice/ (grammar, writing, editing pages), retrieved 2026-08-13. Quotes are his words.

## Structure and voice

- "The canonical order of thoughts in many languages is first given, then new." Start sentences from what the reader already knows; put the new information at the end.
- "Use the active voice where possible (almost always)."
- "Use 'we' instead of 'I' to refer to the authors of a paper."
- Don't use past or future tense for describing what is coming up in a paper. ("Section 5 discusses...", not "Section 5 will discuss...")

## Pronouns and reference

- "Don't use any more pronouns than you need to: this, it, and others." Repeat the noun.
- He/she constructions: "I hate this construction with a passion." Use plural ("agents ... they") or restructure.
- Over-specified definite descriptions are ungrammatical: "the Figure 3" is wrong; "Figure 3" is right.
- Capitalize internal references: Figure 4, Table 2, Section 3.
- Avoid vague references like "the following figures".

## Words and phrases banned in formal writing

- **very** — "usually content-free and frequently with a weakening effect" (Mark Twain).
- **etc.** — "except in casual writing... It indicates a carelessness of thought."
- **slash constructions** (and/or, w/, 24/7, numeric dates like 8/17) — "A slash is another one of those constructions that indicates a profound carelessness of thought." Write "August 17", "and", "or", "and or both".
- **we feel that** — "Who cares about your feelings?" Write "we claim", "we show", "we argue".
- **centered around / focused around** — "Centered on, not around." "Focused on, not around (it wouldn't be focused then, would it?)."
- Hedging filler: "more like", "sort of", "kind of", "basically", "quite", "really" — cut or replace with a direct assertion.

## Sentence beginnings — strict prohibitions

Never begin a sentence with: a lowercase letter, a numeral, a citation, a bracket or parenthesis. For lowercase identifiers, add a word: "The serviceProvider field refers to...", not "serviceProvider refers to...".

## Numbers and dates

- Spell out numbers under ten ("three brief questions", "thirty-four PhD graduates" — his own resume spells out even large counts of people).
- Numerals for larger exact values (157); commas at thousands (1,024); years without commas (2026).
- Dates in formal prose: "August 17", "April 2023–present". Never 8/17 or 08/17/2026.

## Punctuation and typography

- No contractions in formal work: no we'll, I'll, we've, don't, doesn't, can't, won't. Write "it is", "do not", "I have".
- Serial comma: "List three or more items with commas all the way: 'A, B, and C' but not 'A, B and C' unless B and C together are one thing."
- Space after commas, colons, periods, semicolons — never before.
- En-dash for ranges (11–23, August 2020–May 2025); em-dash without surrounding spaces.
- US quotation convention: periods and commas go inside quotes.
- Use quotes only where essential; when introducing a new term, italicize it instead.
- "Use emphasis fonts, e.g., italics or bold, only for a few words or phrases."
- End captions with periods; sentence case for captions and headings.

## Document hygiene

- "Don't use footnotes. Decide if it is worth stating in the main text; if not, don't say it."
- Sections have zero or two+ subsections, never exactly one. Lists have two or more entries.
- Lowercase discipline names: "computer science", not "Computer Science" — "capitalization makes concepts appear more grand than they are." (Internet, Web, and proper-name adjectives such as Bayesian keep capitals.)
- Spell-check and proofread before sharing — it is "rude to off-load this task on your readers."
- "Don't thank your coauthors!" (in acknowledgments).
