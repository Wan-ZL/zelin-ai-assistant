# Grammar and diction rules (Munindar P. Singh, verbatim where quoted)

Source: https://www.csc2.ncsu.edu/faculty/mpsingh/local/advice/ (grammar, editing, spellings pages), verified 2026-08-18. Quotes are his words. Anything not on his pages is labeled "extension" inline or listed under Extensions at the end.

## Structure and voice

- "The canonical order of thoughts in many languages is first given, then new." Known first, new information at the end.
- "Use the active voice where possible (almost always)."
- "Use 'we' instead of 'I' to refer to the authors of a paper." But: "over-use of either personal pronoun can get tiresome, so replace it by neutral entities, e.g., 'This paper develops a ...' instead of 'We develop a ...'"
- No past or future tense for what is coming up in a paper — write "Section 5 presents a formalization of this hocus pocus"; his rule: get rid of the "have" and the "will".
- "Don't precede an enumeration with an exact count, as in 'the following three points ...'"

## Pronouns and reference

- "Don't use any more pronouns than you need to: this, it, and others." Repeat the noun.
- His/her; he/she: "I hate this construction with a passion." His remedies: "If you do need pronouns, you can say his or her and he or she" — or restructure so no singular pronoun is needed. Plurals last: "plurals often make the rest of the construction harder and the meaning less clear."
- Distributive singular: "instead of saying 'agents play roles' say 'each agent plays a role'".
- Over-specified definite descriptions are ungrammatical: "the Figure 3" is wrong; "Figure 3" is right.
- Capitalize internal references: Figure 4, Table 2, Section 3. Avoid vague references like "the following figures".

## That vs. which, and other connectives

- That/which — his "longstanding admonition": "Learn to distinguish 'that' from 'which.'" His semantics: "P that Q means {x: P(x) and Q(x)}"; "P, which Q means {x: P(x)} and by the way P entails Q." Restrictive → that (no comma); by-the-way claim → which (with comma).
- However: "Don't use 'however' to separate sentence-level clauses." "I like pizza. However, it makes me sleepy." is OK, as is "I like pizza. It makes me sleepy, however." — but "I like pizza, however, it makes me sleepy." is not good.
- "While, when you mean whereas. While is potentially ambiguous, because it also indicates an ongoing activity."
- Few vs. a few: "'Few people attended' means just a few (if any) did, whereas 'A few people attended' means that at least a few did."

## Comparisons and citing in prose

- "Don't use comparators unless you specify a comparison: 'our approach is faster' [than what?]"
- Cite authors by name: "I see no benefit from saying things like 'The authors of [7] show ...' or 'In [7], the authors show that ...'" Write "Singh et al. [11] claim ...". (Extension: nameless-citation subjects are also a classic AI/lazy-writing tell — flag on sight.)

## Words and phrases to avoid

- **very** — "Very (Mark Twain): usually content-free and frequently with a weakening, not a strengthening, effect."
- **actually, basically, really** — "often such adverbs are content free and suggest that the rest of what you said is not actual, basic, or real. Many uses of essentially are the same way."
- **kind of, sort of** — "Kind of, sort of: meaningless."
- **etc.** — "except in casual writing... It indicates a carelessness of thought."
- **slashes** — "A slash is another one of those constructions that indicates a profound carelessness of thought." **And/or** is "a particularly unhelpful (but common) special case of the above. The obvious question is 'is it and or or'?" (Rewrite as "A or B, or both" — extension; Singh gives no rewrite.)
- **we feel that** — "Who cares about your feelings? 'We believe' is better, but the best option is not to add such phrasing if you can live without it."
- **centered around / focused around** — "Centered on, not around." "Focused on, not around (it wouldn't be focused then, would it?)."
- **utilize** — "use 'use' instead".

## Spellings and mass nouns

- "Can not: most times should be cannot" — cannot = not(can(...)); can not = can(not(...)).
- Software is a mass noun — never "a software" or "three softwares".
- "Research—you don't do researches".

## Sentence beginnings

Never begin a sentence with a lowercase letter, a numeral, or a citation ("Don't begin a sentence with a citation."). For lowercase identifiers, add a word: "The serviceProvider field refers to...", not "serviceProvider refers to...".

## Numbers, dates, ranges, hyphens

- Spell out numbers under ten (his negative example: don't say "the agent talks to 3 other agents"); numerals for larger exact values (157); commas at thousands (1,024); years without commas (2026).
- Ranges: "You may write 'E occurred over 2008–2015' or 'E occurred between 2008 and 2015' but not 'E occurred between 2008–2015'".
- "Use hyphens where compounds are used as modifiers, and not as plain nouns, e.g., 'this is a run-time error' and 'this error occurs at run time.'"

## Punctuation and typography

- No contractions in formal work: write "it is", "do not", "cannot".
- Serial comma: "A, B, and C", not "A, B and C" unless B and C together are one thing.
- Space after commas, colons, periods, semicolons — never before.
- "Write 'e.g.,' and 'i.e.,' with commas as here."
- Apostrophes are "for possessives ... but not for plurals. That is, avoid constructions like 'ETD's' when you mean multiple ETDs."
- En-dash for ranges (11–23); em-dash without surrounding spaces.
- US quotation convention: periods and commas go inside quotes. Use quotes only where essential; when introducing a new term, italicize it instead.
- "Use emphasis fonts, e.g., italics or bold, only for a few words or phrases."

## Headings and captions

- Section-level headings are title case: "Capitalize sections, subsections, subsubsections (i.e., make first letter of all big or important words upper case)."
- Sentence case is only for the small stuff: "Use sentence case for paragraph headings and end each heading with a period." "Write all captions in sentence case"; captions end with a period.

## Document hygiene

- "Don't use footnotes. Decide if it is worth stating in the main text; if not, don't say it."
- Sections have zero or two+ subsections, never exactly one. Lists have two or more entries.
- Lowercase discipline names: "computer science" — "capitalization makes concepts appear more grand than they are." (Internet, Web, Bayesian keep capitals.)
- Spell-check and proofread before sharing — it is "rude to off-load this task on your readers."
- "Don't thank your coauthors!" (in acknowledgments).

## Extensions (not on Singh's pages)

- Numeric dates: write "August 17", never 8/17 or 08/17/2026 — follows from his slash rule, though he states no date rule himself.
- Comma splice: two independent clauses joined by a bare comma are an error — use a period, a semicolon, or a conjunction. (Singh's pages state only the "however" case above.)
- Hedging filler beyond his list: "more like" — cut or replace with a direct assertion.
- "We show" / "we argue" as substitutes when an assertion verb is unavoidable — Singh's own advice for "we feel that" is to drop the phrasing, or "we believe".
